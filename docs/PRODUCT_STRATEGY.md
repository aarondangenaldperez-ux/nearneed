# NearNeed product strategy

**Reviewed:** 1 November 2026  
**Market:** Philippines  
**Status:** Strategy hypotheses; not proof of demand

## Executive decision

Do not launch as another nationwide, all-category service directory. The visible Philippine market already includes products that advertise provider discovery, customer job posts, competing offers, verification, chat, booking, and payments.

NearNeed's first advantage cannot simply be "find services nearby" or "post a job and receive quotes." Those are already established product patterns. The defensible opportunity, if customer research confirms it, is to become especially good at helping a person describe a specific, non-standard need and receive a small number of relevant, comparable local offers—with clear expectations and privacy-conscious defaults.

**Do not claim this is unique yet.** It is a hypothesis that must be tested against real alternatives people currently use, including Facebook groups, referrals, search engines, and existing marketplaces.

## Public competitor scan

This is a review of what each product publicly markets on its website, not an independent audit of product quality, actual transaction volume, provider availability, or customer satisfaction.

| Product | Publicly described experience | Strategic implication |
|---|---|---|
| [Phriend](https://phriend.app/) | Provider/service listings, service-specific pricing, photos, schedules, in-app chat and booking; says providers are verified with government ID and selfie, while payments are agreed and made directly to providers. | A polished catalogue, provider verification, and direct payment are already being marketed locally. |
| [KAILA](https://kaila-app.com/) | Customers post a job and compare offers by price, timing, experience, and verified status; chat, progress, and reviews are part of its described flow. | A request-first job board is not a unique differentiator by itself. |
| [SkillMart](https://skillmart.ph/) | Job posting, worker applications, in-app messaging, payment checkout, and service categories. | Marketplace workflow and payment features are already being presented by competitors. |
| [Serbisyo](https://serbisyoprovider.com/) | Service catalogue, provider verification, booking, and provider management. | Competing on a broad home-services catalogue would require a strong supply and trust advantage. |

## What NearNeed should test

### Core problem hypothesis

People with a specific task may not know which service category to search, which local provider can do it, or how to compare offers. Existing alternatives may require asking several groups, messaging providers individually, or translating an unclear need into a standard service listing.

### Positioning hypothesis

**NearNeed helps people describe the job in their own words, then compare relevant offers from providers serving the same area.**

This should become the main product flow only if interviews show that the problem is frequent, painful, and not already solved well by the alternatives people use.

### Initial market focus

Start with one pilot locality and no more than two or three service categories. Do not pick the launch location based only on population size or founder convenience. Select it after buyer and provider interviews show:

- repeated recent demand for the same type of task;
- enough reachable providers willing to respond promptly;
- a current workaround that customers describe as inconvenient;
- acceptable safety and moderation risk;
- the ability to test the service manually before paying to scale it.

Avoid launching every category at once. Categories involving home entry, care of vulnerable people, regulated professional work, or safety-critical repairs need stronger safeguards and should not be first-wave categories without suitable operations and review.

## Validation before expansion

Conduct structured discovery with adults who have recently bought or sold a service. A practical first round is 10 potential buyers and 10 potential providers in one candidate locality. These are interview targets, not a statistically representative sample.

Ask about actual recent behaviour, not hypothetical enthusiasm:

1. What was the last service or task you needed?
2. How did you find someone?
3. How many providers did you contact?
4. What made you trust or reject an offer?
5. What was confusing, slow, risky, or expensive?
6. For providers: where do your last five customers come from, and how do you decide whether to respond to a lead?
7. What would make a request worth responding to?

Record patterns without collecting unnecessary personal details. Do not publish interviewees' identities or private stories.

## Pilot scorecard

Track these events from the first controlled pilot:

- eligible visitors;
- completed, valid requests;
- requests receiving at least one relevant offer;
- median time to first relevant offer;
- number of offers per request;
- accepted offers;
- self-reported completed jobs;
- reports, disputes, cancellations, and suspected scams;
- providers who return and respond to another request.

Do not invent targets before seeing a baseline. Define success thresholds after the first manual pilot and compare cohorts over time. The main outcome is **successfully fulfilled requests**, not page views, registrations, or raw post volume.

## Revenue decision

Do not charge customers to post during validation. Do not sell a provider subscription before proving that the platform delivers repeat, qualified demand.

Potential sequence:

1. **Pilot:** free, manually moderated matching; learn which requests produce real outcomes.
2. **After repeat demand:** test an optional provider subscription for tools and qualified lead access, with clear limits and no promise of guaranteed work.
3. **After trust and completion tracking:** consider a transparent fee on successful transactions only if the platform actually facilitates and supports the transaction.
4. **Later:** clearly labelled sponsored placement, only if it does not distort relevance or imply verification.

Do not make payment processing, escrow, or commissions a first-release dependency. They add compliance, support, refund, fraud, and operational work.

## Trust and privacy principles

- Do not expose a customer's exact address or personal phone number on a public request.
- Use city/municipality-level service areas for early discovery.
- Do not describe a provider as "verified" unless a defined review was actually completed and the badge explains what was checked.
- Give users a way to report suspicious or abusive activity before enabling live transactions.
- Use a small adult-only pilot unless a professionally reviewed guardian-consent process is designed.
- Collect only information needed for a specific feature, explain why it is collected, set retention rules, and provide a way to exercise privacy rights.
- Never put credentials, government IDs, customer records, or private interview notes in this public repository.

The Philippine Data Privacy Act requires lawful, transparent, purpose-limited, proportionate processing and appropriate security measures. See the [National Privacy Commission's Act overview](https://privacy.gov.ph/data-privacy-act/) and [right-to-be-informed guidance](https://privacy.gov.ph/the-right-to-be-informed/). This product note is not legal advice or a compliance certification.

## Decision log

| Decision | Reason |
|---|---|
| Request-first matching remains the lead hypothesis | It aligns with the existing NeedBoard concept, but must be tested against KAILA, SkillMart, social groups, and referrals. |
| One locality first | A marketplace needs local liquidity; nationwide breadth can create empty results and slow responses. |
| No payments in the first pilot | Payments add risk before the matching value is proven. |
| No automatic "verified" badge | Trust labels must reflect an actual, documented review. |
| No public precise location or contact data | Reduces avoidable privacy and personal-safety exposure. |
